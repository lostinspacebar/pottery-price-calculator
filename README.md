# Garnet Pricing Model

A practical pricing guide for Garnet's functional pottery. Use the model as a starting point, then compare the result with similar work and the venue where the piece will be sold.

## Price the piece

Start with a base price, then add or subtract for the piece's qualities:

- **Base:** $20–$25 for functional ware. Use less only for something especially small, simple, and easy to glaze; account for more expensive materials when relevant.
- **Details:** add about $5 for each detail, such as a handle, foot, basic carving or design, or a glaze treatment beyond a basic dip glaze.
- **Size:** add about $5 for each size step above small; larger pieces may warrant more.
- **Personal value:** add $5–$20 if you particularly love the piece and would be happy to keep it if it does not sell.
- **Uniqueness:** add $5–$20 for a unique piece.
- **Popular design:** add $5–$10 for a popular style, such as a travel mug or boob mug.
- **Motivation to sell:** subtract $5–$10 if you especially want the piece to sell.

These are judgment-based adjustments, not mandatory fees. Avoid double-counting the same quality under multiple adjustments.

## Include gallery commission and sales tax

For gallery sales, the listed price needs to cover both the **25% gallery commission** and **8.9% sales tax**. The gallery does not add sales tax on top of the displayed price; both amounts come out of it.

Assuming both percentages are calculated from the displayed price:

```text
maker proceeds before costs = listed price × (1 − 0.25 − 0.089)
                           = listed price × 0.661

listed gallery price = desired proceeds before costs ÷ 0.661
```

For example, to receive $50 before recovering costs, list the piece at about **$75.64**. The commission would be about $18.91 and sales tax about $6.73, leaving $50.00. Confirm the gallery's accounting method and tax rules; use its actual method if it differs from this assumption.

## Check the price before deciding

1. **Research comparable work:** What are similar items selling for? Consider whether the artist is well known and whether the comparison is a gallery or Etsy price.
2. **Consider the market:** Local markets differ; for example, San Francisco prices may be higher than Denver prices.
3. **Consider the occasion and venue:** Gallery prices are usually higher than small-market prices, and holiday pieces may sell for more.
4. **Account for your work and costs:** Include time, materials, glazes, firing, and studio access. Decide what you need to recover before settling on proceeds.
5. **Set three reference prices:** Record your minimum acceptable price, your stretch price, and the price that would make you happiest. For gallery sales, convert the proceeds you want into a listed price using the formula above.

The final price should make sense for the piece, its costs, the market, and what you are willing to accept. The model is a guide, not a substitute for checking comparable sales or the gallery's exact terms.
